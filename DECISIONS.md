## 01. Technician Visit: S06 â€” South Hills Fire Station

### Recommendation

Prioritize **S06 â€” South Hills Fire Station** for a technician visit, focusing on the logger clock, timestamp provenance, and possible recovery of buffered observations.

### Evidence

The maintenance log records a battery replacement and logger reboot on 24 March 2025. Analysis of `gps/S06.nmea` reportedly identified 439 GPRMC sentences in which logger timestamps were approximately six hours behind GPS UTC.

For example, a logger timestamp of 10:10 reportedly corresponds to GPS time of 16:10. The reboot period also contains 36 conflicting duplicate timestamps between 10:10 and 16:00 on the same date.

Together, these observations indicate a potential systematic time-alignment problem affecting multiple channels. The exact source of the discrepancyâ€”logger clock, gateway, timezone configuration, or downstream timestamp processingâ€”has not yet been conclusively established.

If the discrepancy affects the remaining observations, it could distort the timing of diurnal temperature patterns, solar irradiance, and wind measurements.

### Proposed inspection procedure

The technician should:

1. Compare the logger's real-time clock (RTC) against a trusted UTC reference.
2. Inspect firmware, timezone, and timestamp configuration.
3. Determine whether timestamps are generated by the logger, gateway, or ingestion pipeline.
4. Inspect internal storage and determine whether buffered observations can be recovered.
5. Preserve the original records and conflicting duplicates before making changes.

### Confirmation and rejection criteria

- **Confirmed logger-clock fault:** The RTC is offset from UTC, and independent evidence supports the same discrepancy.
- **Possible downstream fault:** The RTC is correctly synchronized, but timestamps differ elsewhere in the data path.
- **Unresolved:** Available evidence cannot distinguish these explanations.

A six-hour timestamp shift is a candidate remediation only if the affected interval, offset direction, date boundaries, and record ordering are independently verified. Conflicting records must be reconciled without silently discarding observations.

### Why S06 over S07 and S02?

**S07 â€” Anemometer replacement**

The reported median wind speed decreased from 8.6 km/h to 1.9 km/h after an anemometer replacement on 26 March 2025, a reduction of approximately 78%. This warrants investigation, but the replacement sensor's calibration characteristics are unknown. A short visit cannot establish a reliable correction for the historical series without suitable reference evidence.

**S02 â€” Pressure-board replacement**

A persistent pressure shift of approximately 10.4 hPa was reported following a pressure-board replacement on 21 March 2025. A reference barometer or validated comparison method is needed before treating the proposed correction as authoritative.

**Decision:** S06 is the strongest initial priority because resolving a common timestamp problem could improve the temporal alignment of several channels. Successful recovery is possible but not guaranteed.

## 03. Where AI Assistance Was Wrong

AI-assisted development accelerated prototyping, but generated suggestions and preliminary detection results required critical review. The examples below describe the failure modes encountered during development; implementation-specific claims must be checked against the final committed code.

### Example 1: Treating extreme wind observations as faulty spikes

**Initial approach**

A proposed approach applied a single-point modified robust z-score to numerical channels, including `wind_kmh`, and recommended excluding observations that crossed a statistical threshold.

**Why this was problematic**

Wind speed is naturally variable and can exhibit genuine short-lived gusts, particularly during mountain-front weather events. A statistical outlier is not necessarily a sensor failure.

During an earlier development iteration, the generated flags reportedly included 46 false positives involving legitimate wind gusts of 35â€“60 km/h.

**How the issue was identified**

Inspection of the flag manifest, surrounding observations, and cross-station behavior suggested that many flagged measurements could represent real atmospheric events rather than isolated instrument faults.

**Engineering lesson**

Anomaly detection should be channel-aware. Statistical extremeness is useful as a screening signal, but it should not automatically justify exclusion. Physical limits, temporal context, neighboring stations, and sensor behavior should inform the final action.

Any claimed change to the spike-detection implementation should be verified against the final `src/anomalies.py`.

### Example 2: Confusing regional pressure changes with maintenance-related faults

**Initial approach**

Broad maintenance-keyword matching was considered as a way to associate abrupt level shifts with hardware interventions.

**Why this was problematic**

S06 reportedly experienced a median pressure increase of approximately 3.9 hPa after 24 March. Its maintenance record describes a battery replacement, not a pressure-board replacement.

Neighboring stations S04, S05, and S07 reportedly experienced pressure increases of approximately 3.6â€“4.6 hPa over the same period. This cross-station behavior is consistent with a regional atmospheric change and weakens the claim that S06's shift was caused by its battery replacement.

**How the issue was identified**

Cross-station comparison showed that the pressure shift was not isolated to S06. Temporal coincidence with maintenance was insufficient evidence of a hardware-induced pressure offset.

**Engineering lesson**

Maintenance correlation should support a diagnosis, not replace evidence of a physical cause. A stronger implementation should combine event-specific maintenance matching with cross-station comparisons and should preserve uncertainty when the cause cannot be established.

The final report should describe only the maintenance-matching logic that is actually present in the committed implementation.

## 04. Scaling the System to 500 Stations

Scaling should focus on configuration, reliable processing, useful alerts, and measured performanceâ€”not distributed infrastructure for its own sake.

### 1. Configuration-driven rules

Move thresholds into a schema-validated configuration file, such as YAML. Rules can then be adapted to station characteristics such as alpine ridge, urban basin, or agricultural valley.

Configuration changes should be versioned and auditable so that each finding can be traced to the rules used to produce it.

### 2. Spatial and temporal baselines

Use rolling temporal baselines and comparisons with nearby stations, while accounting for elevation and local environmental differences.

Spatial consensus can help distinguish a regional weather event from a station-specific fault. However, nearby stations should not automatically be treated as interchangeable references.

### 3. Incremental processing

Process new observations in batches rather than repeatedly reprocessing the entire history. A message broker such as Apache Kafka could support ingestion, while a time-series store such as TimescaleDB or ClickHouse could support historical queries.

A target such as sub-50-ms processing per micro-batch should be treated as a benchmark requirement, not an assumed result.

### 4. Root-cause alert grouping

Group related channel anomalies under a station-level incident when evidence supports a common cause. For example, a verified logger-clock fault may explain several channel-level timing anomalies.

Grouping should reduce redundant alerts without removing the underlying observations or diagnostic evidence.

### 5. Human-in-the-loop triage

Route high-impact `keep` and `correct` findings to a review queue. Technicians should be able to inspect supporting evidence, approve or reject proposed corrections, and record the decision.

Original measurements and audit history should remain available after remediation.

### 6. Capacity planning and distributed processing

Assuming 500 stations each produce one record every ten minutes:

- 500 Ã— 144 = **72,000 station-time records per day**.
- At eight sensor channels per record, that is approximately **576,000 channel observations per day**.
- Over 30 days, this is approximately **17.28 million channel observations**.

A modern single-machine Python pipeline using tools such as Polars or DuckDB may be sufficient for this workload. Actual processing time and memory consumption should be benchmarked using representative data.

Distributed processing frameworks such as Spark or Ray become more attractive if the network expands substantially, raw measurements arrive at high frequency, or processing becomes computationally intensive.

**Decision:** Scale according to measured bottlenecks, reliability requirements, and operational needsâ€”not station count alone.

## Final Engineering Principles

The main lessons from this project are:

1. **Preserve evidence:** Do not silently discard conflicting records before their provenance and contents have been examined.
2. **Separate detection from diagnosis:** A statistical anomaly is a reason to investigate, not proof of a faulty sensor.
3. **Use contextual evidence:** Maintenance logs, GPS records, neighboring stations, and physical constraints can strengthen or weaken a suspected diagnosis.
4. **Treat corrections cautiously:** Preserve original data and verify the correction's direction, scope, and assumptions before applying it.
5. **Make uncertainty explicit:** A defensible `keep` decision can be better than an unjustified `exclude` or `correct`.
6. **Benchmark before scaling:** Use measured performance and resource consumption to determine when more complex infrastructure is warranted.

These principles guide the interpretation of the generated flags and the operational recommendations in the HTML report.
